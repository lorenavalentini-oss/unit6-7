Reading Text

People often assume that photographs show reality exactly as it happened. However, digital technology has made that assumption much less reliable. Images can be edited within seconds, and artificial intelligence can now create convincing pictures of events that never took place. This does not mean that visual information is useless. Photographs remain powerful because they can communicate complicated ideas immediately. A single image can attract attention, create an emotional reaction and encourage people to learn more about an issue. The difficulty is deciding how much to trust. Experts recommend checking the source, comparing the image with reports from other organisations and thinking about the reason why it was published. Context matters too. A genuine photograph can still create a misleading impression if important information is left out. Stories online create a similar problem. An exciting narrative can spread quickly even when it is inaccurate. Readers therefore need to ask not only whether a story is interesting, but also whether the claims can be supported by reliable evidence. Being critical does not mean distrusting everything. It means taking enough time to examine information before accepting or sharing it.

Writing 1 · Personal Blog Post (140–190 words)

Write about yourself: Think of a real time when something you saw — a photograph, video, post, documentary, advert or news story — changed your opinion about a person, place, animal, environmental issue or social topic.

Explain what you saw, what you believed before, how your viewpoint changed, and what you think now. Try to use Unit 6 language such as perspective, influence, environmental, dramatically, in the middle of, sooner or later and at least one passive structure.

This is a personal response and is not automatically graded. Word count is included in the final result panel.

Writing 2 · A True Story About You (140–190 words)

Write a true story from your own life. Describe a conversation or event in which somebody advised, warned, persuaded, invited, reminded, encouraged or recommended something to you — and explain what happened afterwards.

Use at least three reporting verbs, one example of reported speech, two Unit 7 phrasal verbs, and descriptive story language such as unexpected twist, memorable, special effects, final scene where appropriate.

Personal response — not automatically graded.
